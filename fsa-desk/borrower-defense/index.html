Borrower Defense Loan Discharge

When a school misled you — discharge of the loans tied to that school

Official resources

  • Apply for Borrower Defense
  • Sweet v. McMahon settlement
  • Borrower Defense updates — group discharge announcements
  • Sweet Exhibit C school list (PDF)

Borrower Defense hotline: 1-855-279-6207

“My school misled me. Is there anything I can do?”

Yes — borrower defense. A borrower who enrolled, or kept attending, based on a school's misleading information or other misconduct covered by the regulation, and who suffered a detriment warranting full discharge, can apply to have the applicable federal loans discharged.

“enrolled in a school or continued to attend a school based on misleading information from the school or other misconduct covered by the regulation”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“Which loans can be discharged?”

Federal loans that are Direct Loans or that can be consolidated into a Direct Consolidation Loan — including FFEL, Perkins, and PLUS loans. Private loans, and loans that can't be consolidated into a Direct Consolidation Loan, are not covered.

“don’t apply to private student loans or loans that can’t be consolidated into a Federal Direct Consolidation Loan”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“How do I apply?”

Online with the Apply tool on StudentAid.gov/borrower-defense, or on the paper form (OMB No. 1845-0163). Budget about three hours for one school, including preparation. Gather first: FSA ID, school name(s) and program of study, enrollment dates, and documentation showing why they qualify and the harm they suffered.

“An application for one school takes approximately three hours including preparation time.”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“Which rules decide my application? Why is it taking so long?”

The newest borrower defense regulation (published November 1, 2022) is under a federal court injunction issued August 7, 2023 and reinforced April 4, 2024 — the Department will not adjudicate applications under that enjoined rule while the injunction stands. Borrowers may still apply online, and the Department continues to adjudicate applications.

“While this injunction is in effect, borrowers may still apply online for borrower defense relief.”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“I got a ‘revise and resubmit’ notice — I'm in the Sweet settlement.”

They must submit a NEW application through the page, referencing their previous Borrower Defense Application Number from the notice. Emailing supplemental information instead is treated as failing to revise and resubmit. The Sweet v. McMahon settlement (final approval November 16, 2022; effective January 28, 2023) covers applications filed on or before November 15, 2022.

“you will be treated as having failed to revise and resubmit”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“I heard my whole school got a group discharge — how does that work?”

ED has approved group discharges for specific schools (cards below). Relief is automatic: borrowers in an announced group do NOT need to apply or take any action — ED notifies them by email. While discharges are processed, their loans remain in forbearance / stopped collections; when processed, remaining balances are discharged, payments made to ED on those loans are refunded, and the credit tradelines are deleted.

“Borrowers do not need to take any action to receive their discharge.”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“What's the Sweet settlement school list (Exhibit C)? Do I get automatic relief?”

Class Members — borrowers whose borrower defense application was pending as of June 22, 2022 — whose loans paid for a school on the settlement's Exhibit C list receive Full Settlement Relief automatically, with no new application: the loans are discharged, amounts paid to ED are refunded, and the credit tradeline is deleted. The settlement only covers applications filed on or before November 15, 2022; ED notifies borrowers in this group. The school list itself is a PDF linked from the settlement page (see Official resources above).

“the federal student loan(s) associated with the borrower's attendance at the school will be discharged”— the site’s own words, source page

“Ashford University — was there a group discharge?”

Yes. Borrowers who enrolled in Ashford University (now the University of Arizona Global Campus) between March 1, 2009 and April 30, 2020 receive 100% discharges of their eligible federal student loans, automatically — about 261,000 borrowers, $4.5 billion. Announced January 15, 2025.

“will receive 100% discharges of their eligible federal student loans”— the site’s own words, source page

“Center for Excellence in Higher Education (CEHE) schools?”

Yes. Borrowers who enrolled at any CEHE school — Independence University, CollegeAmerica, Stevens-Henager College, California College San Diego — between January 1, 2006 and August 1, 2021 receive 100% discharges automatically — about 73,600 borrowers, over $1.15 billion. Announced January 13, 2025.

“Drake College of Business?”

Yes. Borrowers who enrolled at Drake College of Business between January 1, 2008 and its closure on July 31, 2015 receive 100% discharges automatically — about 11,000 borrowers, $107 million. Announced January 13, 2025.

“Lincoln Technical Institute?”

Yes, a narrow one: borrowers in the criminal justice program at the Lowell, Massachusetts campus (enrolled 2010–2012) or the Somerville, Massachusetts campus (enrolled 2010–2013) receive 100% discharges automatically — about 280 borrowers, $1.4 million. Announced January 13, 2025.

“The Art Institutes?”

Yes. Borrowers who enrolled at The Art Institutes between January 1, 2004 and October 16, 2017 receive 100% discharges automatically — nearly 317,000 borrowers, over $6.1 billion. Announced May 1, 2024.

“CollegeAmerica in Colorado?”

Yes. ED automatically discharges all remaining federal student loans for borrowers who enrolled at CollegeAmerica brick-and-mortar campuses in Colorado on or after January 1, 2006 and before July 1, 2020 — about 7,400 borrowers, about $130 million. Announced July 25, 2023.

“Westwood College?”

Yes. ED discharges all remaining federal student loans for borrowers who enrolled at any Westwood College location between January 1, 2002 and November 17, 2015 — including borrowers who never applied for borrower defense — about 79,000 borrowers, nearly $1.5 billion. Announced August 30, 2022.

Yes — the broadest one. Group discharge for all students with federal student loans who attended a school owned or operated by Corinthian Colleges, Inc. from its founding in 1995 to its closure in April 2015, whether or not they ever submitted a borrower defense application — about 560,000 borrowers, 100% discharges, about $5.8 billion. “What happens while my application is being reviewed?”

Determinations are sent by email, and status can be checked under My Activity in the StudentAid.gov dashboard. A forbearance / stopped-collections period runs with the application: it ends for remaining loans once eligible loans are discharged — and ends for all loans if the application is denied.

“the forbearance or stopped collections period for any of your remaining federal student loans will end”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“My claim was approved — what do I get?”

The loans tied to the application are discharged; the borrower stays responsible for any other loans. They may also receive a refund of payments previously made on the Direct Loan (or the loan repaid by the Direct Consolidation Loan). Refunds are processed through the U.S. Treasury, returned the way they were received, typically one to two weeks after the discharge completes — and may be offset by other federal debts (such as taxes or child support). The servicer confirms whether a refund applies.

“may receive a refund of payments you previously made on the Direct Loan”— the site’s own words, source page

“My claim was denied. Now what?”

THE SITE DOESN’T SAY — gap on the source page

No discharge is granted, the forbearance / stopped-collections period ends for all of their loans, and the borrower is responsible for repaying them. Honest gap: the page itself states no appeal or reconsideration path — don't invent one; that's a question to route by team practice.

“If we deny your borrower defense application, you won’t receive a discharge of any of your federal student loans”— the site’s own words, source page
