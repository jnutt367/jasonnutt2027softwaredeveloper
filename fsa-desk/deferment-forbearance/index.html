Deferment & Forbearance

Pausing payments — the nine deferments, forbearance, and what interest does meanwhile

Official resources

  • Temporary relief hub
  • Deferments
  • Forbearance

Requests run through your loan servicer (their website, the type's form, or by phone for hardship)

“How do I request a deferment or forbearance — all my options?”

Three doors, all ending at your loan servicer: ① ONLINE — you may be able to apply for certain deferments and forbearances on your servicer's website. ② FORM BY MAIL OR EMAIL — identify which type you're requesting, fill out that type's form, gather the documents that show you meet its requirements, and send both to your servicer. Each type has its own form (Cancer Treatment, Economic Hardship, Unemployment, General Forbearance, the Mandatory Forbearance forms, and more). ③ PHONE — contact your servicer if you're experiencing financial hardship. Most types are NOT automatic — the in-school deferment is the big exception.

“You may be able to apply for certain forbearances and deferments on your servicer's website”— the site’s own words, source page

“What's the difference between a deferment and a forbearance?”

Interest. With a deferment you make no payment, and interest doesn't accrue at all on some loan types (subsidized loans — next card). With a forbearance you make no payment — or a temporarily smaller one — but you're responsible for the interest on everything. The hub's warning on forbearance: you have a limited amount available — save it for when you really need it.

“The difference between deferment and forbearance has to do with interest accrual (accumulation)”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“I sent the form — can I stop paying now?”

No — you must continue making payments until you've been notified your deferment (or forbearance) has been granted. If you stop paying and it isn't approved, your loans become delinquent and you may go into default.

“You must continue making payments on your student loan(s) until you have been notified that your request for deferment has been granted”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“Does interest build up during a deferment?”

Depends on the loan. You are NOT responsible for interest on: Direct Subsidized Loans, Subsidized Federal Stafford Loans, Federal Perkins Loans, and the subsidized portions of Direct and FFEL Consolidation Loans. You generally ARE responsible on: Direct Unsubsidized, Unsubsidized Stafford, Direct PLUS, FFEL PLUS, and the unsubsidized portions of consolidation loans. If you're responsible, you can pay the interest as it accrues, or let it accrue and be capitalized — added to principal — which raises the total you'll repay.

“During a deferment, interest doesn't accrue on some types of loans”— the site’s own words, source page

“Which deferments are about school or service?”

IN-SCHOOL: enrolled at least half-time at an eligible school — in most cases placed automatically; you may opt out. If it doesn't happen automatically, contact your school, which reports your enrollment status. Graduate/professional Direct PLUS borrowers get an additional six months after dropping below half-time. PARENT PLUS BORROWER: while the student you borrowed for is enrolled at least half-time, plus an additional six months after. GRADUATE FELLOWSHIP: enrolled in an approved graduate fellowship program. REHABILITATION TRAINING: enrolled in an approved rehabilitation training program (vocational, drug abuse, mental health, or alcohol abuse rehabilitation). MILITARY: on active duty in connection with a war, military operation, or national emergency; the post-active-duty deferment runs until you resume half-time enrollment or 13 months after the completion of active duty and any grace period, whichever is earlier. All deferments are available to Direct, FFEL, and Perkins borrowers; Perkins also carries a six-month post-deferment grace period in most cases.

“in most cases your loans will be placed into a deferment automatically”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“Which deferments are about hardship or health?”

UNEMPLOYMENT: you receive unemployment benefits, or you're seeking and unable to find full-time employment — up to three years. ECONOMIC HARDSHIP: up to three years — qualifying routes include receiving a means-tested benefit like TANF, working full-time with income not above the greater of the federal minimum wage rate or 150% of the poverty guideline for your family size and state, or serving in the Peace Corps. CANCER TREATMENT: while you're undergoing cancer treatment and for the six months after it ends. OBBBA cutoff the page states: economic hardship and unemployment deferments won't be available for loans made on or after July 1, 2027.

“You can receive this deferment for up to three years”— the site’s own words, source page

“What is a general (discretionary) forbearance?”

Your servicer DECIDES whether to grant it — it's for when you're temporarily unable to make scheduled payments: financial difficulties, medical expenses, a change in employment, or other reasons acceptable to your servicer. Available for Direct, FFEL, and Perkins loans. Length: for loans received before July 1, 2027, no more than 12 months at a time; for loans on or after July 1, 2027, up to nine months within a 24-month period. There's a cumulative limit of three years on general forbearances — when one expires and the hardship continues, you may request another.

“Your loan servicer decides whether to grant a request for a general forbearance”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“What is a mandatory forbearance?”

If you meet the requirements, your servicer is REQUIRED to grant it. Six types: AmeriCorps service with a national service award · DoD Student Loan Repayment Program · medical or dental internship/residency (specific requirements live on the form, not the page) · National Guard duty activated by a governor when you're not eligible for a military deferment · student loan debt burden — payments at 20% or more of your total monthly gross income, for up to three years · teaching service that would qualify for Teacher Loan Forgiveness. Granted no more than 12 months at a time and renewable while you remain eligible; the page states no cumulative cap. The mandatory types apply to Direct and FFEL loans unless noted (debt burden also covers Perkins).

“If you meet the eligibility requirements for a mandatory forbearance, your loan servicer is required to grant the forbearance”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“What happens to interest during a forbearance?”

You're responsible for the interest that accrues while you're not making payments, on every loan type. After the forbearance ends, you pay the accrued interest off through normal monthly payments — for most loan types it does NOT capitalize. The exception: unpaid interest DOES capitalize after a forbearance on FFEL loans not managed by the U.S. Department of Education.

“If you get a forbearance, you're still responsible for the interest that accrues while you're not making payments”— the site’s own words, source page
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“Do deferment or forbearance months count toward forgiveness?”

Generally no. The deferment page: in most cases a deferment will not allow you to make progress toward PSLF or IDR forgiveness. The forbearance page is plainer: forbearance will not allow you to make progress. (Some deferment types DO count for PSLF — they're carded in this aid's PSLF area.) The hub's suggested alternative for longer relief is an income-driven plan — in some cases your payment could be as low as $0 per month, and $0 IDR months still count.

“you'll stop making progress toward forgiveness until you resume repayment”— the site’s own words, source page
