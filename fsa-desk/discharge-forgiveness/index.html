“What are ALL the ways a federal loan can be discharged, canceled, or forgiven?”

The hub names: Total and Permanent Disability Discharge · Public Service Loan Forgiveness · Military Service Benefits · Federal Perkins Loan Teacher Cancellation · Teacher Loan Forgiveness · Closed School Loan Discharge · Borrower Defense Loan Discharge · False Certification Discharge · Unpaid Refund Discharge · Discharge Due to Death · Forgery Discharge. PSLF, Borrower Defense, TPD, and Closed School each have their own area in this aid; the rest are carded on this page. The standing rule behind all of them: if you don't qualify for loan discharge, cancellation, or forgiveness, you must repay your loan — whether or not you completed your education, found a related job, are happy with the education, or were a minor when you signed the promissory note.

  • The hub notes that the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) made significant changes to the federal student aid programs, and that the page has been updated with the latest information.

“How do I apply for Teacher Loan Forgiveness? (all my options)”

★ THE ROUTE — one door, through your loan servicer: submit a Teacher Loan Forgiveness Application to your loan servicer AFTER you have completed the required five consecutive years of qualifying teaching service. The chief administrative officer of the school or educational service agency where you performed the service must complete the application's certification section. If your loans are with different loan servicers, you must submit a separate form to each of them. Form help: 1-888-303-7818 (the discharge & forgiveness support line).

“How much can Teacher Loan Forgiveness forgive — and for whom?”

Up to $17,500 if you were a highly qualified full-time mathematics or science teacher who taught at the secondary school level, OR a highly qualified special education teacher (elementary or secondary level) whose primary responsibility was providing special education to children with disabilities, teaching children whose disabilities corresponded to your area of special education training. Up to $5,000 if you were a highly qualified full-time elementary or secondary education teacher in another subject. The amount is a COMBINED maximum across Direct Loan and FFEL Program loans. The service behind it: teach full time for five complete and consecutive academic years in a low-income school or educational service agency, and meet the other qualifications (see the conditions card).

“What are the Teacher Loan Forgiveness conditions and exclusions?”

Balance rule: you must not have had an outstanding balance on Direct Loans or FFEL Program loans as of Oct. 1, 1998, or on the date that you obtained a Direct Loan or FFEL Program loan after Oct. 1, 1998. Service: five complete and consecutive academic years as a full-time, highly qualified teacher, at least one of those years after the 1997–98 academic year; the school or educational service agency must be listed in the Teacher Cancellation Low Income (TCLI) Directory; the loans must have been disbursed before the end of your five academic years. Default: if you're in default on a loan, you are not eligible for forgiveness of that loan unless you have made satisfactory repayment arrangements with the holder. NOT eligible: PLUS loans for parents and graduate or professional students, and Federal Perkins Loans. No double credit: the same period of service cannot count toward both TLF and PSLF — and once you receive forgiveness, ED can't make changes. A partial-year exception exists: at least half the academic year completed, your employer treating your contract as fulfilled for salary/tenure/retirement, and the interruption caused by a condition covered under FMLA, return to postsecondary education in an area directly related to the teaching service, or being called to active duty for more than 30 days as a reservist.

“What is a False Certification discharge?”

You may be eligible for a discharge of your Direct Loan Program or FFEL Program loan if, for certain reasons, you weren't eligible to receive the loan but the school certified your loan anyway. Four categories: (1) High School Graduation Status — the school certified your eligibility even though you reported that you didn't have a high school diploma or the equivalent. (2) Disqualifying Status — at the time of certification, you had a status that disqualified you from meeting the legal requirements for employment in your state in the occupation the program was preparing you for. (3) Identity Theft — a loan in your name was falsely certified as a result of the crime of identity theft committed against you. (4) Unauthorized Signature or Unauthorized Payment — an employee or affiliate of the school signed your name on the loan application or promissory note without your authorization, or the school endorsed your loan check or authorized an electronic transfer without your authorization for purposes other than paying charges you owed.

“you weren't eligible to receive the loan, but the school certified your loan anyway”— the site’s own words, source page

“How do I apply for a False Certification discharge?”

Complete the loan discharge application that applies to your circumstance — there are four: the False Certification Loan Discharge Applications for High School Graduation Status, Disqualifying Status, Identity Theft, and Unauthorized Signature/Payment. Group route: state attorneys general and nonprofit legal services representatives can use the Group Loan Discharge Application: False Certification of Student Eligibility or Unauthorized Payment to submit a request on behalf of a group of borrowers. The forgery split: complete the Unauthorized Signature/Payment application only if a school employee or affiliate signed your name; if someone else forged your signature and you don't meet the identity-theft requirements, you may qualify for a Forgery Discharge instead (available for Direct Loans, and for FFEL and Perkins Loans held by ED). For more information, contact your loan servicer.

“My school kept loan money it should have returned — what's the Unpaid Refund discharge?”

If you withdrew from school after receiving a Direct Loan Program or FFEL Program loan, the school may have been required to return some or all of the loan money to your loan servicer. If the school didn't make a required return, you might be eligible for a discharge of the portion the school failed to return — and ONLY that portion will be discharged. Parent PLUS counts: if your child withdrew and the school didn't make a required return of your parent PLUS loan funds, the amount that should have been returned will be discharged. Order of operations from the page: if the school is still open, contact the school and attempt to resolve the issue before applying; if the school has closed, first determine whether you may be eligible for a closed school discharge instead. The formal route: complete the Loan Discharge Application: Unpaid Refund and send the completed form to your loan servicer.

“Only the portion of your loan that your school should have returned will be discharged”— the site’s own words, source page

“A borrower has died — what happens to the loans?”

Federal student loans will be discharged due to the death of the borrower or of the student on whose behalf a PLUS loan was taken out — a parent's PLUS loan is discharged if the parent dies, or if you (the student) die. How it happens: a family member or other representative provides the loan servicer acceptable documentation of the death — an original death certificate, a certified copy of the death certificate, or an accurate and complete photocopy of one of those documents. No application form is named; contact the loan servicer for its documentation requirements.

“Federal student loans will be discharged due to the death of the borrower”— the site’s own words, source page

“Can federal student loans be discharged in bankruptcy?”

In some cases — but discharge in bankruptcy is not an automatic process. You must file for bankruptcy and file a separate action, known as an adversary proceeding, requesting the bankruptcy court find that repayment would impose undue hardship on you and your dependents. Factors a court may look at: if you're forced to repay, you would not be able to maintain a minimal standard of living · there is evidence the hardship will continue for a significant portion of the loan repayment period · you made good faith efforts to repay the loan before filing bankruptcy. Possible outcomes: your loan may be fully discharged and all collection activity stops · it may be partially discharged, with some portion still to repay · or you may be required to repay with different terms, such as a lower interest rate. Your creditors may be present to challenge the request. (The page also notes that on March 9, 2022, ED asked the Department of Justice to request a pause of any active bankruptcy case if the borrower wishes.)

“discharge in bankruptcy is not an automatic process”— the site’s own words, source page

“What about Perkins Teacher Cancellation, Military Service Benefits, and Forgery Discharge?”

The hub names all three programs; this aid does not yet carry their own pages, so don't improvise their rules at the desk — their official pages govern. What the hub itself states: if you're a Federal Perkins Loan borrower, you may be eligible to have all or a portion of your Perkins Loan canceled or discharged (the Federal Perkins Loan Teacher Cancellation appears on the hub under several borrower sections). The false certification page adds one boundary for Forgery Discharge: it is available for Direct Loans and for FFEL Program loans and Perkins Loans that are held by the U.S. Department of Education.
