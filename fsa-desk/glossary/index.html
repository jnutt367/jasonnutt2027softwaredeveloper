Plain-English glossary

The acronyms and terms an agent meets every day — in words you can read aloud on a call. Every meaning below is built from the site’s own definitions; where the site puts it best, its words are quoted and linked, exactly as the cards do.

Verified against the site October 28, 2026 · main source: the Financial Aid Dictionary and the program pages named on each entry

FAFSA — Free Application for Federal Student Aid

The form you submit to apply for federal student aid — grants, loans, and work-study.

“Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA®) form”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — FAFSA hub
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

FSA ID

Your StudentAid.gov account username and password. You use it to log in, complete forms, and sign electronically — and it is how the site knows it is you.

Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

SAI — Student Aid Index

An index number calculated from the information on your FAFSA form. Your school subtracts it from its cost of attendance to figure out how much need-based aid you can get.

“It’s NOT the amount of money that you or your family will have to pay for school”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

COA — Cost of Attendance

The total estimated cost of attending a school for one year — tuition and fees, housing and food, books and supplies, transportation, and loan fees.

“The total cost of attendance (COA) includes expenses related to food, housing, supplies and other living expenses, tuition and fees”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Contributor (on the FAFSA)

A person — a parent or a spouse — who must contribute their information and signature to a student’s FAFSA form when the student is a dependent student or is married.

“Contributors include the student’s parent(s) or spouse.”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

FAFSA Submission Summary

The paper or electronic document you receive after your FAFSA form is processed. It holds the information you entered and your SAI.

“The record of what you entered on the FAFSA® form”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Federal Pell Grant

A grant — money you usually do not repay — for undergraduate students with exceptional financial need who have not earned a bachelor’s, graduate, or professional degree.

“A Pell Grant is not a loan, so it does not have to be repaid”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Grant

Student financial aid that generally does not have to be repaid — most often awarded based on financial need.

“A form of financial aid that usually does not need to be repaid”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Federal Work-Study

A federal program that gives undergraduate and graduate students the chance to earn money for their education costs through part-time employment.

“provides students with the opportunity to earn money to help pay for their education-related expenses”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Direct Loan

A student loan made by the U.S. Department of Education to undergraduate, graduate, and professional students and to parents.

“a student loan made by the U.S. Department of Education”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

FFEL — Federal Family Education Loan Program

The older federal loan program. Its loans (Stafford, PLUS, and Consolidation loans made under it) still sit in repayment beside Direct Loans — the site’s plan descriptions list FFEL loans as eligible alongside Direct Loans.

Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Subsidized Loan

A need-based Direct Loan on which the U.S. Department of Education pays the interest while you are in school at least half-time, during the grace period, and during deferment periods.

“The U.S. Department of Education pays the interest on a Direct Subsidized Loan”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Unsubsidized Loan

A Direct Loan on which the borrower is responsible for paying all the interest that accrues, at all times.

“The borrower is responsible for paying all the interest”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

PLUS Loan

A Direct Loan for eligible graduate or professional students and for parents of dependent undergraduate students.

“available for eligible graduate or professional students and for parents of dependent undergraduate students”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

MPN — Master Promissory Note

The legal promise-to-repay you must sign before Direct Loan funds can be paid out. One MPN can cover Direct Subsidized and Unsubsidized Loans for up to 10 years.

“serves as a promise from the borrower to repay all loans, interest, and fees”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Loan Servicer

The company a federal loan is assigned to after the loan is made. It handles billing and other services on the loan — payments, repayment plans, and forgiveness options run through it.

“handles the billing and other services on a federal student loan”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Consolidation

Combining one or more federal education loans into a single new Direct Consolidation Loan — afterward there is one loan servicer and one monthly payment.

“consolidate (combine) one or more federal education loans into a new Direct Consolidation Loan”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Direct Consolidation Loans
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Grace Period

For Direct Subsidized and Unsubsidized Loans, the six-month period after you graduate, leave school, or drop below half-time enrollment — before you begin repayment.

“this six-month period is called the grace period”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Interest Capitalization

When unpaid interest is added to your loan’s principal balance. After that, interest is charged on the higher balance.

“interest capitalization occurs when unpaid interest is added to the principal balance”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Deferment

A temporary delay of repayment while certain conditions are met. On a Direct Subsidized Loan, the federal government generally pays the interest during the deferment.

“Deferment is a period during which the repayment of the principal and interest of your loan is temporarily delayed”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Forbearance

A temporary stop or reduction of required payments for up to 12 months at a time while certain conditions are met. Unlike deferment, interest accrues during every forbearance period.

“Interest accrues during all types and periods of forbearance”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Default

What happens when required payments are not made. For federal student loans, default typically occurs when a required payment is not made for more than 270 days. (The desk’s Default page carries the consequences and the ways out.)

“default typically occurs when a required payment isn’t made for more than 270 days”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Discharge / Forgiveness / Cancellation

Being released from the obligation to repay your loan. The site treats discharge, forgiveness, and cancellation as essentially the same thing.

“discharge, forgiveness, and cancellation mean essentially the same thing”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Standard Repayment Plan

The plan that pays the loan back in fixed monthly payments over a 10-year term. It is the plan a borrower is entered into if they do not choose another repayment plan.

“pays back a borrower’s loans over a 10-year repayment term”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

IDR — Income-Driven Repayment

A type of repayment plan that sets your monthly payment amount from your income and family size — and requires a yearly recertification to stay on it.

“bases your monthly student loan payment amount on your income and family size or number of dependents”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Income-Driven Repayment Plans
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

PSLF — Public Service Loan Forgiveness

Forgives the remaining balance on your Direct Loans after you have made the equivalent of 120 qualifying monthly payments under an accepted repayment plan, while employed full-time by an eligible employer.

“the equivalent of 120 qualifying monthly payments”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF)
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

TPD — Total and Permanent Disability Discharge

A discharge for a borrower who is totally and permanently disabled. If it is granted, the remaining balance of the loan does not have to be repaid.

“you do not have to repay the remaining balance of the loan”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Total and Permanent Disability (TPD) Discharge
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Borrower Defense (BD)

A discharge a borrower can apply for when they enrolled, or continued to attend, based on a school’s misleading information or other misconduct covered by the regulation, and suffered a detriment warranting full discharge of the applicable loans.

“enrolled in a school or continued to attend a school based on misleading information from the school or other misconduct covered by the regulation”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Borrower Defense Loan Discharge
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

TEACH Grant

The Teacher Education Assistance for College and Higher Education Grant — up to $4,000 a year for students completing coursework to begin a career in teaching, in exchange for a teaching service agreement. (Fail the agreement and the grant converts to a loan.)

“provides grants of up to $4,000 a year to students who are completing or plan to complete course work needed to begin a career in teaching”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — TEACH Grant Program
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Entrance Counseling

Counseling required before a student can receive a Direct Subsidized or Unsubsidized Loan for the first time — it explains your rights and responsibilities as a borrower.

“required before a student can receive a Direct Subsidized Loan or a Direct Unsubsidized Loan for the first time”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Exit Counseling

Guidance a school must give its loan borrowers when they graduate, leave school, or drop below half-time — what you owe and what your repayment options are.

“Exit counseling is mandatory for all Direct Subsidized Loan, Direct Unsubsidized Loan, and Direct PLUS Loan borrowers”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

SAP — Satisfactory Academic Progress

The standards a student must meet to keep receiving federal student aid — a specified grade-point average and successful progress toward the degree or certificate, evaluated at least annually.

“requires students to maintain a specified grade-point average (GPA) and progress successfully toward completing a degree or certificate program”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Financial Aid Dictionary
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

Two terms the site does not define here

AGI and NSLDS carry no definition in the Financial Aid Dictionary read October 28, 2026, so — by this desk’s rule — they are not defined here either. Income questions live on the Income-Driven Repayment pages; a borrower’s aid history lives on My Aid. When the site defines them, they will be added with the source named.
