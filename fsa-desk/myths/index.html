Myth vs. site

Things borrowers believe — some of them from old news, some from wishful thinking — answered by the site’s own sentences. Each row: the belief in the borrower’s words, the plain answer, the site’s own words quoted short, and the desk card that carries the full picture.

Drawn from this desk’s own cards, verified as their pages state · built October 28, 2026. A belief ships here only when a sentence on StudentAid.gov settles it.

“Fresh Start is still open — I can use it to get out of default.”

No. Fresh Start ended on October 2, 2024; the site mentions it only as history. The live routes out of default are the three the site names now: pay in full, rehabilitation, or consolidation.

“if you enrolled in the Fresh Start program (which ended on Oct. 2, 2024)”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Student Loan Default and Collections: FAQs

“If I just wait, my default will go away on its own.”

It won’t. Staying in default changes nothing by itself — the site names exactly three ways out (pay in full, rehabilitation, consolidation), and waiting is not one of them. Meanwhile the balance, collection costs, and credit damage keep growing.

“If you stay in default, you may experience involuntary collections like wage garnishment and Treasury offset until your debt is paid in full or the default is resolved”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Student Loan Default and Collections: FAQs

“The government is already taking my tax refund and garnishing my wages.”

Not right now. Per the site’s Collections page, Treasury offset and Administrative Wage Garnishment are currently paused — there is no withholding at this time. The machinery is real, and the notices and hearings that come with it are carded on the Collections page for when it is active.

“PSLF means 120 payments in a row, with one employer — change jobs and you start over.”

Neither half is true. The payments need not be consecutive — time with a non-qualifying employer pauses the count; it does not erase it — and full-time hours can be combined across qualifying employers.

“make a total of 120 qualifying monthly payments that need not be consecutive”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Public Service Loan Forgiveness

“If I die, my spouse or my family will have to pay my student loans.”

No. Federal student loans are discharged when the borrower dies — the debt does not pass to a spouse or family. A parent’s PLUS loan is likewise discharged if the parent dies, or if the student it was borrowed for dies. A family member simply gives the servicer documentation of the death; no application form is named.

“Federal student loans will be discharged due to the death of the borrower”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Death Discharge

“Consolidating will lower my interest rate.”

It can’t. The new loan’s rate is the weighted average of the rates on the loans you combine — rounded up, never down. Consolidation rearranges the loans; it does not discount them.

“weighted average of the interest rates on the loans being consolidated, rounded up to the nearest one-eighth of one percent”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Loan Consolidation

“Consolidating has no downside — it’s always worth it.”

The site names a real one: you may lose borrower benefits you already have — interest rate discounts, principal rebates, some cancellation benefits. And a consolidation cannot be undone, so the trade is permanent.

“you may lose certain borrower benefits—such as interest rate discounts, principal rebates, or some loan cancellation benefits”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Loan Consolidation

“A TEACH Grant is a grant no matter what — it can never become a loan.”

It can — automatically, if the teaching service isn’t completed. Every TEACH Grant received converts to a Direct Unsubsidized Loan, with interest charged from the date each grant was paid out, not from the conversion date.

“all TEACH Grants you received will be converted to Direct Unsubsidized Loans”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — TEACH Grant Program
Open the source on StudentAid.gov ↗

“My school is on the Sweet settlement list, so my loans will be forgiven automatically.”

Only for a defined group. Automatic Full Settlement Relief belongs to Sweet Class Members — borrowers whose borrower defense applications were already pending as of June 22, 2022 — whose loans paid for a school on the Exhibit C list. Everyone else applies for borrower defense and is reviewed; attendance at a listed school alone forgives nothing.

“the federal student loan(s) associated with the borrower’s attendance at the school will be discharged”— the site’s own words, StudentAid.gov — Sweet Settlement
